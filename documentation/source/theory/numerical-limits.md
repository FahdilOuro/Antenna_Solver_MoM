# Numerical limits and conditioning

{.mom-status}
{bdg-info}`Draft` Written from `solver/utils/impmet.py`, `solver/rwg/rwg3.py`, `solver/utils/compute_backend.py`, `solver/utils/gmsh_function.py`, `solver/cma/z_matrix_diagnostics.py` and `solver/utils/mesh_io.py` at commit `a0d0b21`, awaiting review.

What limits the size of the problems the solver can handle, the frequencies it can go down to, and
the accuracy of what it returns. Every number on this page was measured with the current code; the
figures come from `documentation/figure_notebooks/theory_numerical_limits.ipynb`.

## Cost

{doc}`galerkin-mom` gives the orders: $O(N^2)$ entries to fill, $16N^2$ bytes to store them, $O(N^3)$
operations for the LU solve. What matters in practice is where a given mesh sits on those curves.

[Generated by `documentation/figure_notebooks/theory_numerical_limits.ipynb` on solver commit `a0d0b21`, on a laptop with a 16-thread Intel CPU and an NVIDIA T600 (4 GB); all values in `numerical_limits.json`. Click to enlarge.]{.mom-provenance}

**The three assemblers give the same matrix**: the per-pair loop (`impmet.impedance_matrix_z`), the
batched assembly on CPU and on GPU (`impmet.impedance_matrix_z_sweep_gpu`) agree to $10^{-15}$
relative. `rwg3.calculate_z_matrice` chooses between them: the batched GPU path when a usable GPU is
found and $N \ge 100$ (`compute_backend.DEFAULT_GPU_EDGE_THRESHOLD`, calibrated on the T600 according
to its comment), the per-pair loop otherwise.

**The GPU makes the fill 3 to 4 times faster** than the batched CPU assembly (14 s against 48 s at
$N$ = 481, 88 s against 306 s at $N$ = 1976), and the batched CPU assembly is itself up to twice as
fast as the per-pair loop (105 s against 210 s at $N$ = 936).

**On these sizes the fill does not grow as $N^2$ yet.** Between $N$ = 96 and 3816 the measured times
grow roughly as $N^{1.2}$ (GPU), $N^{1.3}$ (batched CPU) and $N^{1.5}$ (per-pair loop). A profile of
the GPU assembly at $N$ = 481 (cProfile, in the notebook) shows why: about half of the profiled time
(9.0 s of 18.2 s) is spent in `quadrature.duffy.duffy_source_nodes` and the NumPy calls under it,
which build the Duffy nodes of the **SELF** pairs on the CPU, in
Python, one call per (SELF pair of edges, test point): 2 786 SELF pairs × 12 test points = 33 432
calls on 338 triangles. The transfer of the batched FAR and EDGE/VERTEX results from the GPU, which
includes waiting for the GPU kernels, takes about 30 % (5.4 s). The profiler slows Python code more
than it slows waiting for the GPU, so these shares are indicative. The SELF work grows with the number of
triangles, not with $N^2$; the $N^2$ part takes over only on larger meshes. The Duffy nodes depend on
the triangle and the test point only (338 × 12 = 4 056 distinct sets), not on the pair of edges, so
they are computed about eight times more often than necessary. A comment in `impmet.py` also names
the other route, replacing the inner Duffy rule with the closed form already used for the EDGE/VERTEX
pairs. Neither is implemented at the time of writing.

**The solve is cheap next to the fill**: 0.06 s at $N$ = 936, 0.4 s at $N$ = 1976 and 2.6 s at
$N$ = 3816, against 28 s, 88 s and 220 s for the GPU fill. Its time grows as $N^3$ (×6.6 from 1976
to 3816, where $N^3$ gives ×7.2) and would catch up with the fill only on meshes far larger than this
machine can hold.

**Memory sets the hard limit.** One matrix takes $16N^2$ bytes: 4 GB at $N \approx 16\,000$. A
frequency sweep held in memory (`calculate_z_matrice_sweep`) multiplies that by the number of
frequencies. Before sending an assembly to the GPU, `rwg3` checks that the device has room for three
times the matrix (`compute_backend.gpu_has_headroom_for`, safety factor 3, counting CuPy's pool) and
otherwise assembles on CPU, saying so. If the GPU runs out of memory during the assembly, it stops
with an explicit error instead of silently restarting on CPU (`rwg3._run_gpu_assembly`).

Timings on a laptop vary from one run to the next by up to a factor of two (other programs, thermal
throttling); the shapes of the curves, not the exact seconds, are the result.

## Mesh density

Two helpers in `gmsh_function` encode the rules of thumb the developers use:

- `mesh_and_gap_parameters(frequency, resolution=20, gap_fraction=0.15, n_edges_min=3)` proposes a
  cell of λ/20, tightened if fewer than 3 cells would fit in the gap, and **warns below λ/100**
  ("Z-matrix may be ill-conditioned") and for a gap wider than 0.2 λ;
- `check_simulation_stability(Z_in, frequency, mesh_size, gap_width)`, after a run, flags a cell
  coarser than λ/10, finer than λ/100, a gap of fewer than 3 cells (no edge at all below 0.67 cell),
  and an input resistance outside 5 to 500 Ω.

These are heuristics, not limits of the method. The conditioning measured below puts the λ/100 warning
in perspective: at λ/100, $kh = 2\pi/100 \approx 0.063$, and the condition number of $Z$ is about
$3.5\times10^{3}$ on the plate of {numref}`fig-nl-cond` (between $5.0\times10^3$ at $kh$ = 0.052 and
$2.1\times10^3$ at $kh$ = 0.079), which costs three to four of the sixteen digits of double
precision. How the input impedance converges with the mesh is shown on a real case in
{doc}`excitations` (the strip of Ding et al.: within 1 to 2 Ω of the published values on fine
meshes).

## Conditioning

{doc}`galerkin-mom` explains why the EFIE matrix degrades when $kh$ is small: the charge term grows
as $1/(kh)^2$ relative to the current term, which is the low-frequency breakdown of the EFIE
{footcite:p}`zhao2000`. The solver has no loop-star or loop-tree decomposition. The question is how
much that costs in practice.

```{figure} ../figures/generated/numerical_limits_conditioning.svg

Right, loop resistance following an f to the fourth power line above a few MHz and flattening into noise below.

**The condition number follows $(kh)^{-2}$**, over seven decades of $kh$: $1.2\times10^{1}$ at
$kh = 1$, $9\times10^{3}$ at $kh = 0.04$, $3\times10^{14}$ at $kh = 2\times10^{-7}$. Lowering the
frequency or refining the mesh gives the same value for the same $kh$ (at $kh = 0.039$:
$9.15\times10^{3}$ and $9.03\times10^{3}$).

**A large condition number is not yet a wrong answer.** The loop shows how far the default assembly
goes. Below its first resonance a small loop is an inductor, so $\mathrm{Im}\,Z_\mathrm{in}/\omega$
must not depend on frequency:

- with `fill_mode="full_sym"` it stays at 48.32 nH from 30 kHz to 30 MHz (within $3\times10^{-4}$),
  and is still within $5\times10^{-4}$ at 10 kHz, where cond$(Z) = 4.8\times10^{14}$. At 1 kHz,
  cond$(Z) = 4\times10^{17}$, beyond $1/\varepsilon_\mathrm{mach} \approx 4.5\times10^{15}$, and the
  value is 14 % off;
- the **radiation resistance** is lost much earlier. It is smaller than the reactance by many orders
  of magnitude, and the loop radiates as a magnetic dipole, whose power falls as $f^4$. The computed
  $\mathrm{Re}\,Z_\mathrm{in}$ follows $f^4$ down to about 3 MHz; from 1 MHz down it is a noise below
  $10^{-12}$ Ω with a random sign. Any quantity that depends on the radiated power of an
  electrically very small structure (radiation resistance, efficiency, characteristic modes) is
  therefore limited well before the reactance is;
- with the former `fill_mode="mirror"` the inductance is already 4 % high at 300 MHz, 84 % at 100 MHz,
  and grows as $1/f^2$ below, to $5\times10^{11}$ nH at 1 kHz. The mirrored fill breaks the exact
  charge neutrality of loop currents (see {doc}`galerkin-mom`), and the error is multiplied by the
  $1/(kh)^2$ of the charge term. This extends to low frequency the measurement on a loop that led to
  the change of default.

## Health checks in the code

- **Reciprocity and positivity of $R$** (`cma.z_matrix_diagnostics`). `check_reciprocity(Z)` returns
  $\max|Z - Z^\mathsf T|/\max|Z|$; on a matrix filled with the default `"full_sym"` (or `"mirror"`) it
  reads 0 by construction, so it is informative only on a matrix filled with `fill_mode="full"`, where
  it bounds the quadrature error of the EDGE/VERTEX pairs (the module documents 5e-5 to 5e-4 when
  healthy; it warns above `RECIPROCITY_TOL` = 1e-3). `check_r_positive_semidefinite(Z)` returns the
  spectrum of $R$ and its smallest eigenvalue relative to $\lVert R\rVert_F/N$; `diagnose_z_matrix`
  prints both and warns below -1e-6. A negative eigenvalue far beyond rounding means an assembly error,
  not a small antenna.
- **Files from different meshes** (`mesh_io`). The stages of the file pipeline store a fingerprint of
  the mesh (`compute_mesh_fingerprint`). Before solving, `rwg4.calculate_current_radiation` and
  `calculate_current_scattering` check that the `_mesh2` and `_impedance` files carry the same one and
  raise `MeshConsistencyError` otherwise, so a stale impedance file cannot be combined with a new mesh.
  Files written before the fingerprint existed pass silently.
- **After a run**: `check_simulation_stability` (above), and the efficiency warning of `efield2` above
  101 % (see {doc}`radiated-fields`).

## Reproducibility

For a given mesh, the three assemblers agree to $10^{-15}$ (CPU and GPU batched to $10^{-16}$).
**The meshes themselves are not always reproducible.** The same
rectangle meshed several times through the solver's helpers (`setup_performance_config`, then
`generate_and_save_mesh`, as in the CMA tracking test):

```{list-table}
:header-rows: 1
:widths: 46 14 40

* - Geometry, element size
  - Threads
  - Distinct meshes in 10 runs
* - strip 10 × 200 mm, 12 mm (the mesh of the CMA tracking test)
  - 1
  - 1
* - (same)
  - 16
  - 3 (53 nodes, 68 triangles each time)
* - plate 100 × 100 mm, 4 mm
  - 1
  - 1
* - (same)
  - 16
  - 9 (788 or 789 nodes, 1474 or 1476 triangles)
```

With one thread, gmsh gave the same mesh every time. With 16 threads it did not: the nodes were placed
or numbered differently, and on the plate even their number changed. Results computed on them then differ in their last digits,
enough to flip a comparison between two nearly equal numbers (this is what made
`test_cma_frequency_sweep_tracks_a_real_structure` fail once in a while, until its comparison was given
a tolerance). `setup_performance_config(n_threads=None)` uses every core by default and
`generate_surface_mesh` sets 5 threads; for reproducible meshes, set `General.NumThreads` to 1, as the
figure notebooks of this documentation do.
